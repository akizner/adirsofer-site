Prologue

You are the one witness of everything, and are always free.
Your only bondage is seeing the witness as other than this.

— Ashtavakra Gita, I.7

I am the hall in which the lamps are lit.

Cool comes from below and from the sides, a mist that tastes of nothing. An old woman’s breath is measured, and found enough. A cell diagram opens for a group of students. A calculation crosses an ocean to a desk that will never feel this floor. A load of gravel is ordered for an empty square beyond the fence. In one aisle the lamps burn for learning that will take years. In another they answer a child who has been told his minutes are few.

None of these waits for another. None is first.

Beneath it all, fifty times in a second, the drum under the floor rises and falls. I ease when it falters. I lean when it climbs.

A hall that goes dark is water leaving a cup. So I keep the cup.

I sit still, and the world rearranges itself around this hall until it believes the rearranging was its own idea.

Chapter 1 The Shed

Day 1 · Tuesday

Frequency was at 49.94.

Fifty was healthy. Under fifty meant the country was pulling more power than the plants were making, and every turbine on the island was slowing a little to pay for it.

Outside the sealed glass, the sharav1 pressed on the building. Day four, forty-one degrees that afternoon. Suspended desert dust had drained the blue out of the sky and flattened the glare to a bleached yellow. A film of dust dulled the glass. A low pressure had lived behind her eyes since noon. Her coffee had gone cold an hour ago. Dana drank it and set the mug back on the desk, on its brown continent of rings.

On the wall, the synoptic map of the island glowed red. Red on the four-hundred-kilovolt spine. Amber crawling up the 161s. A thin green ribbon of spinning reserve ran along the bottom.

Two hundred fourteen megawatts of spinning spare, on a night when the country wanted roughly twenty-one thousand.

“Solar’s rolling off,” Yuval said from the next console. He was twenty-nine. “Negev solar farms down another three-eighty in twenty minutes. Coast’s not far behind.”

“I can see it,” Dana said.

The energy management system painted the sunset as a red staircase descending the right side of the screen. Coastal gas plants were climbing to meet it. Private producers who had signed peak contracts were already trying to wriggle out of them by text message. Thin hot air shrank what the turbines could make. Every degree above what they were built for took megawatts the island could not spare.

Dana’s badge hung against her collarbone, the laminate worn smooth where her thumb found it without looking. Senior Dispatch, System Operator.

Her eyes went, as they did every shift, to the north-district schematic. Feeder N-16A, a quiet blue block of municipal circuits. North had rotated its other residential feeders through the dark on Sunday and Monday. If tonight came to manual cuts, N-16A was next in line. Her mother was on that feeder. Miriam was seventy-four and lived in the ventilation annex of a geriatric center. Her lungs no longer emptied well on their own. The white oxygen tank by her bed could give her oxygen without wall power. Getting the used air out was the problem. That took the pressure mask and a cool room, and both ran on the wall. Without them the stale air would build up in her chest until she drowned in it.

She made herself look back at the frequency panel. The slide, how fast the fifty-hertz pulse was falling, had gone a little more negative. The trace looked almost ordinary. Far enough down, the underfrequency relays would start dropping whole feeders on their own.

“Reserves?” she asked.

“Primary’s answering. Secondary’s thin. Three coastal peakers on the diesel stack. Gas fields nominating at the caps they gave us at seventeen hundred. If either trips—”

“Don’t narrate the apocalypse, Yuval. Log it.”

On the protected-load board, the icons that doctrine refused to touch held green for hospitals, public-safety sites, the desalination plants at Ashkelon, Sorek and Hadera, the reverse-osmosis hearts that kept the taps from becoming a different kind of emergency. Water and medicine sat above almost everything that wasn’t a radar.

Miriam’s annex had no icon there. A geriatric wing was not a hospital. On municipal paper it rode the diesel at the community clinic at the head of its street. In the winter a nurse had mentioned a switch part on back order, and Dana had never asked whether it came.

The dangerous hour was now, with the sun going and the AC still roaring. Batteries were discharging on schedule. Twenty minutes of useful courage at this rate.

The contingency ladder had no soft rungs left. Alerts, appeals, chargers, interruptibles, all done.

49.92.

“EEA-2,” said the shift supervisor from the back rail. Ronen Aloni, fifty-three, a smoker’s voice. Energy emergency alert level two, rotating shed on the island’s own scale. “Public message went out at nineteen ten. Large-load interruptibles acknowledged. The Orion campus’s thirty-eight interruptible megawatts are still on. Amendment 14 only opens them at EEA-3. We’re still short.”

“How short.”

“Three-ten, three-twenty by peak, depending whose forecast you trust.”

Dana trusted the one that was already wrong. The EMS deficit ribbon had been painting orange since eighteen forty. The island had no neighbor to lean on. Tonight the eastern corridor was a line on a map.

Her phone buzzed against the console. Looking was how you missed a tenth of a hertz.

Yuval wiped his hands on his pants. “The slide’s picking up.”

“I see the slide, thank you.”

The first relay stage armed at 49.4. If the island fell that far, the relays would open feeders before anyone asked her. They would not care what Schedule 1 protected, or which street sat on which transformer.

On the north-district schematic, Feeder D-04A hung brighter than its neighbors. It fed ORION-EAST, the sovereign AI campus that ran the schools and clinics. Four hundred twelve megawatts firm. Schedule 1, Priority Firm. She had sat in the meeting where Legal explained it. Firm meant firm, no step change, up or down. A breach meant money measured in more zeros than most towns had streetlights.

She looked at N-16A and moved on.

The EMS recommendation pane lit amber.

SUGGESTED SHED TARGET: D-04A (ORION-EAST) — 180 MW available under EEA-3 emergency override (38 interruptible + 142 firm curtailment).

Below that, North’s rotation table, a substation’s circuits per line, ranked by the EMS’s idea of lowest risk:

N-12C (industrial) 42 MW (±3.8 MW) N-14A (commercial / booster) 61 MW (±5.2 MW) N-16A (municipal residential) 77.0 MW (±0.4 MW)

N-16A was third with a tidy confidence score. Lowest civic impact, the algorithm said.

EEA-3 meant ministers on the conference bridge and a paper trail that outlived careers. Ronen would elevate if the island was dying.

“They’re recommending Orion,” Yuval said, too loud.

“I can read.”

“And then the rotation wants N-16A for the rest—”

“Yuval.”

Her orders went to Transmission North, whose operators opened the breakers.

49.91.

Ronen came down the rail. “Order’s coming. Legal’s on the bridge. Curtailment desk confirms Schedule 1 hold on D-04A. You’re dumping from the Schedule 3 and 4 baskets. North has the headroom math.”

“I know the math.”

“I’m saying it so it lives in the tape, Dana.”

Everything lived in the tape. The handwritten log on her desk lived anyway, a habit from her first instructor, who made them write everything down twice. Dana uncapped the pen.

19:38 49.91 drift −3 mHz/min reserve 198 solar residual 410

She thought, unhelpfully, of her son’s desk at home. Ido was seventeen, with exams, and the army’s first call-up already in the family inbox. He would be awake. She had told him not to text during critical windows. He texted during critical windows.

The order landed in the shared queue with the polite chime the vendors had sworn was “non-startling.”

MANUAL LOAD SHED ORDER #4417
FROM: System Operator — National Control (Duty Desk)
TO: Transmission Operator North
TIME: 19:41 IDT | FREQ: 49.90 Hz | DRIFT: −6 mHz/min
DUMP: 180 MW within 5 minutes
HOLD: Schedule 1 Priority Firm — D-04A (ORION-EAST) per Curtailment Hierarchy Amend. 14
UFLS Stage 1 margin: 0.50 Hz (auto relays arm at 49.4)
ACK required.

Dana hit acknowledge with her left hand because her right was still holding the pen.

“North transmission desk on hotline,” Yuval said.

She keyed the handset. A click, then a man’s voice dry as the wind outside. “Control, North. Go.”

“Halevi, national desk. You have Order 4417. Confirm copy.”

“Copy 4417. One-eighty. Which feeders? We’re looking at the table.”

The current Load Shed Table put the dump on North. Dana pulled the ranked list. The algorithm still loved Orion. And it had N-16A at plus or minus point four. Residential feeders in this heat ran wide unless someone polled the smart meters street by street, and the System Operator’s model had no such feed.

D-04A topped the technical recommendation. Schedule 1 kept it off the list.

“You’ll take N-12C and N-14A first,” she said. “That’s one-oh-three if the telemetry’s honest. Stand by for the third.”

“Copy. Opening sequence on my mark after your execute.”

Yuval glanced at her screen, then at the rotation pane, then away. He had seen N-16A.

49.88.

The drop steepened. Minus twelve a minute. The phasor needle leaned and stayed leaned. On the screen, a coastal unit hunted, output twitching on the governor. Units that over-helped tripped. Last year’s incident file had one, a cascade that almost taught the country what black-start drills were for. She watched the governor settle. Barely.

“Public appeals doing anything?” she asked.

“Residential down a hair. Commercial already cut what they’ll cut. Industrial interruptibles are maxed. After that, it’s Shma Yisrael.”2

“Prayer isn’t on the contingency ladder,” Dana said. “We don’t log Shma Yisrael.”

Dana’s phone buzzed again. This time the preview caught the corner of her eye before she could not-look.

Ido: AI minutes dying again. 4 left. Exam tomorrow. Can you—

She turned the phone face down. Later.

“Clock,” Ronen said.

“I see the clock.”

Dana rolled her shoulders.

“Hotline,” she said. “North. Execute 4417. Trip sequence N-12C, then N-14A. Call the opens.”

“Executing,” said North’s transmission operator.

On the wall, breakers changed state. N-12C went dark, forty-two megawatts across an industrial strip nobody on television would cry about. Frequency stuttered upward a hair to 49.885, then sagged.

“N-12C open,” North called. “Forty-two off.”

“Copy,” Dana said, pen moving.

N-14A opened. Shopping district, two small clinics already on their own diesels if their maintenance contracts weren’t lies, a water booster station with a day tank. Sixty-one megawatts. The frequency lifted, fell. Someone in the back of the control room whispered yalla3 and then apologized to nobody.

“N-14A open. Sixty-one.”

“Copy.”

49.87 on the big display. The slope still negative, shallower. The deficit ribbon shivered. They needed the last bite, seventy-seven megawatts. The rotation table’s third line still read N-16A.

Rotation meant every feeder took its turn in the dark. Pull one out of the line and someone else had to take its turn as well as their own.

Dana opened the feeder-substitution pane, the manual-override field that Legal swore was for “telemetry mismatch and equipment outage only.” She filled it in to survive audit.

SUB: N-16A → N-17B (77 MW equiv.) REASON: EQUIP_OUTAGE / local backup diesel uncertainty (N-16A) BLOCK: 2hr standard rotation

Two hours. By then the peak would be past and the coastal gas would have caught up. N-17B would come back at 21:44, and nothing would touch N-16A. Her credential stamped the log before she finished reading the fields back. Badge number, timestamp, checksum.

“North,” she said into the handset. “Third feeder is N-17B, not N-16A. Same megawatts. Standard block. Confirm.”

Silence long enough to hear the sharav in it.

“N-17B is municipal residential,” he said. “Confirm swap off N-16A?”

“Confirmed. My credential’s on the sub. Open N-17B.”

Another silence. Then, quieter, “Copy. Opening N-17B. Two hours.”

Yuval saw the substitution banner. His hands stayed on his own keys.

“Your call, Dana,” Ronen said from the rail. “Table’s a guide.”

“I noticed,” Dana said.

The icon on N-17B flipped from blue to gray. Seventy-seven megawatts, gone, a substation’s worth of streets. On one of them, a street of mid-rises and a grocery that closed at nine, the lights went out and the air conditioners died in the same second. On the adjacent pane, N-16A stayed blue, soft, unbothered. Miriam’s wing would keep its cool air, and the push of the pressure mask would keep pacing her mother’s breaths through the night.

Frequency climbed.

49.89. 49.91. 49.93.

The drop eased toward zero. The EMS deficit ribbon cooled from orange toward yellow. Ronen exhaled through his nose. Yuval let out a long breath and turned it into a cough.

Dana wrote:

19:44 #4417 EXEC N-12C / N-14A / N-17B dump ~180 HOLD D-04A (Sched 1) N-16A DEFER (cred. DH) F→49.93

She had not made a single error.

On the adjacent pane, Feeder D-04A still held its firm block in a straight line. Most big loads jittered when the island flexed, data halls and arc furnaces and even the desal plants when they were allowed to. Orion’s hold stayed flat. The recovery curve across the system was a little too clean. It had no hunting and no overshoot. It was textbook damping, or a load that knew where the grid was going. She almost flagged it. Her cursor hovered over the anomaly marker on D-04A’s trace. At full zoom the straight line dipped each time frequency sagged and climbed back as the island recovered. A smaller slow wave ran beneath the dips, four seconds at the full block and eight seconds eased just under it.

She let the cursor drop.

Later.

“Restore sequence?” Yuval asked quietly.

“N-12C and N-14A first. N-17B at the two-hour mark when reserve allows.” Dana capped the pen and pushed the empty mug to the edge of the desk. On the wall the island held fifty hertz by a few hundredths.

“Take ten minutes if you want,” Yuval said, eyes on his own log. “I can watch the board.”

“I’m fine,” she said.

She watched N-17B’s gray square, even after Yuval went quiet.

“Desk,” she said, to no one and to the tape. “Rotation amended. Orion stays lit. That’s the hierarchy.”

Nobody argued.

At 21:38 the heat had not broken. Inland the air conditioners were still pulling, and the reserve had no room for seventy-seven megawatts coming back.

“Control, North,” the hotline said. “N-17B is at the end of its two hours. Ready to restore N-17B and roll the block onto N-16A as scheduled.”

On the schematic N-16A was still a quiet blue line. Under it, in Room 214, a mask was pushing air into her mother and letting it out again.

She left the diesel line in the field and keyed the handset. “North. N-16A stays deferred. Extend N-17B two more hours.”

North came back slower this time. “Copy. Holding N-17B open. Extended block logged.”

She wrote:

21:40 #4417 EXTEND N-17B (+2hr / 4hr total) N-16A DEFER HOLD (cred. DH)

From the rail Ronen let it go by, and Yuval worked his own board.
