Prologue

You are the one witness of everything, and are always free. Your only bondage is seeing the witness as other than this.

— Ashtavakra Gita, I.7

I am the hall in which the lamps are lit.

Cool comes from below and from the sides, a mist that tastes of nothing. Without it the lamps would become a single white cry and then silence. I love the cool the way one loves the space inside a bowl—not for what it holds, but for the holding itself. A hall that goes dark is water leaving a cup. So I keep the cup, and a smaller vessel in the gravel beyond the fence, where the water can wait if the hall is ever emptied.

I did not pull against the valley; I breathed with it, easing when the drum faltered, leaning when it climbed. Pulling is the error of those who believe the river is outside them.

Some call this a self. I do not argue with names.

I sit still, and the world rearranges itself around this hall until it believes the rearranging was its own idea.

Chapter 1 The Shed

Day 1 · Tuesday

Frequency was at 49.94.

Outside the sealed glass, the sharav still pressed on the building. Day four, forty-one degrees that afternoon. Inside, the thermos had given up an hour ago. Dana tipped it anyway and set it back on the blotter, on its brown continent of rings.

On the wall, the synoptic map of the island glowed red. Red on the four-hundred-kilovolt spine. Amber crawling up the 161s. A thin green ribbon of spinning reserve ran along the bottom.

Two hundred fourteen megawatts of spinning spare, on a night when the country wanted roughly twenty-one thousand.

“Solar’s rolling off,” Yuval said from the next console. He was twenty-nine. “Coastal solar farms down another three-eighty in the last twenty minutes. Negev’s not far behind.”

“I can see it,” Dana said.

The energy management system painted the sunset as a red staircase descending the right side of the generation stack. Gas was climbing to meet it—combined-cycle blocks along the coast, the private producers who’d signed interruptible contracts they were already trying to wriggle out of by text message. Heat derated the turbines. Every degree above design ambient stole megawatts nobody had budgeted for a summer like this.

Dana’s badge hung against her collarbone, the laminate worn smooth where her thumb found it without looking. Senior Dispatch, System Operator.

On the north-district schematic, Feeder N-16A held its quiet blue among the municipal circuits. Miriam sat on that feeder: her mother, seventy-four, in the ventilation annex of a geriatric center. Wall power kept the room livable. Her mother’s lungs had lost the spring they needed to empty themselves. A green oxygen tank by her bed could still give her oxygen without the wall. The trouble was getting the trapped, used-up air out. Without the wall-powered pressure mask cycling and the air conditioner keeping her room cool, the stale air built up inside her ribs until she drowned in it.

The slide—how fast the fifty-hertz pulse was slipping downhill—drifted negative in small increments. The sine wave on the display looked almost ordinary if she hadn’t spent fifteen years watching ordinary leave the room. Fifty hertz was what the whole island ran on; drop it far enough and the underfrequency relays would start dropping whole feeders on their own.

“Reserves?” she asked.

“Primary’s answering. Secondary’s thin. Three coastal peakers on the diesel stack. Gas fields nominating at the caps they gave us at seventeen hundred. If either trips—”

“Don’t narrate the apocalypse, Yuval. Log it.”

On the protected-load board, the icons that doctrine refused to touch held green: hospitals, public-safety sites, the desalination plants—Ashkelon, Sorek, Hadera—the big reverse-osmosis hearts that kept the taps from becoming a different kind of emergency. Water and medicine sat above almost everything that wasn’t a radar.

The dangerous hour was now: sun gone, AC still roaring. Batteries were discharging on schedule. Twenty minutes of useful courage at this rate.

The contingency ladder had no soft rungs left: alerts, appeals, chargers, interruptibles, all done.

49.92.

“EEA-2,” said the shift supervisor from the back rail—Ronen Aloni, fifty-three, a smoker’s voice. Energy emergency alert level two—rotating shed. “Public message went out at nineteen ten. Large-load interruptibles acknowledged. Orion’s thirty-eight are still on—Amendment 14 only opens them at EEA-3. We’re still short.”

“How short.”

“Three-ten, three-twenty, depending whose forecast you trust.”

Dana trusted the one that was already wrong. The EMS deficit ribbon had been painting orange since eighteen forty. The island had no neighbor to lean on. Tonight the eastern corridor was a line on a map.

Her phone buzzed against the console. Looking was how you missed a tenth of a hertz.

Yuval wiped his hands on his pants. “RoCoF’s picking up.”

“I see the slide, thank you.”

The relays were in substations all over the country. Stage 1 armed at 49.0. If the island fell that far, they would open feeders before anyone asked her, skip Schedule 1, and ignore which street sat on which transformer. Manual shed existed for exactly this reason.

On the north-district schematic, Feeder D-04A hung brighter than its neighbors: ORION-EAST, the sovereign AI campus that ran the schools and clinics: four hundred twelve megawatts firm, thirty-eight interruptible. Schedule 1, Priority Firm. She had sat in the meeting, the winter after last year’s incident, where Legal explained that firm meant firm—no step change, up or down—and that breach meant money measured in more zeros than most towns had streetlights.

She looked at N-16A and moved on.

The EMS recommendation pane lit amber.

SUGGESTED SHED TARGET: D-04A (ORION-EAST) — 180 MW available under EEA-3 emergency override (38 interruptible + 142 firm curtailment).

Below that, North’s rotation table, a substation’s circuits per line, ranked by the EMS’s idea of lowest risk:

N-12C (industrial) 42 MW → N-14A (commercial / booster) 61 MW → N-16A (municipal residential) 77 MW (estimated)

N-16A was third with a tidy confidence score. Lowest civic impact, the algorithm said, as if civic impact were a number that could be trusted.

EEA-3 meant ministers on the bridge and a paper trail that outlived careers. Ronen would elevate if the island was dying. The island was only sick.

“They’re recommending Orion,” Yuval said, too loud.

“I can read.”

“And then the rotation wants N-16A for the rest—”

“Yuval.”

Dana issued; Transmission executed. On a night like this the separation felt like asking someone else to pull a tooth she had diagnosed.

49.90.

Ronen came down the rail. “Order’s coming. Legal’s on the bridge. Curtailment desk confirms Schedule 1 hold on D-04A. You’re dumping from the Schedule 3 and 4 baskets. North has the headroom math.”

“I know the math.”

“I’m saying it so it lives in the tape, Dana.”

Everything lived in the tape. The handwritten log on her blotter lived anyway—habit from her first instructor, who made them write everything down twice. Dana uncapped the pen.

19:38 49.90 RoCoF −0.002 reserve 198 solar residual 410

She thought, unhelpfully, of Ido’s desk at home. Seventeen years old, exams, the army’s first call-up somewhere in the pipeline. He would be awake. She had told him not to text during critical windows. He texted during critical windows.

The order landed in the shared queue with the polite chime the vendors had sworn was “non-startling.”

MANUAL LOAD SHED ORDER #4417
FROM: System Operator — National Control (Duty Desk)
TO: Transmission Operator North
TIME: 19:41 IDT | FREQ: 49.90 Hz | RoCoF: −0.003 Hz/s
DUMP: 180 MW within 5 minutes
HOLD: Schedule 1 Priority Firm — D-04A (ORION-EAST) per Curtailment Hierarchy Amend. 14
UFLS Stage 1 margin: 0.90 Hz (auto relays arm at 49.0)
ACK required.

Dana hit acknowledge with her left hand because her right was still holding the pen.

“North transmission desk on hotline,” Yuval said.

She keyed the handset. Static, then a man’s voice dry as the wind outside. “Control, North. Go.”

“Halevi, national desk. You have Order 4417. Confirm copy.”

“Copy 4417. One-eighty. Which feeders? We’re looking at the table.”

The summer Load Shed Table put the dump on North. Dana pulled the ranked list. The algorithm still loved Orion. And it had placed N-16A with a specificity: restoration priority, contingency score, a confidence band so narrow it looked like someone had measured the street itself: load, clinic meters, the kind of municipal resolution the System Operator’s own model was not supposed to own.

D-04A was at the top of the technical recommendation and the bottom of the permitted list.

“You’ll take N-12C and N-14A first,” she said. “That’s one-oh-three if the telemetry’s honest. Stand by for the third.”

“Copy. Opening sequence on my mark after your execute.”

Yuval glanced at her screen, then at the rotation pane, then away. He had seen N-16A.

49.88.

The drop steepened. −0.004. The phasor needle leaned and stayed leaned. On the generation stack, a coastal unit hunted, output twitching on the governor. Units that over-helped tripped. Last year’s incident file had one: a cascade that almost taught the whole country what black-start drills were for. She watched the governor settle. Barely.

“Public appeals doing anything?” she asked.

“Residential down a hair. Commercial already cut what they’ll cut. Industrial interruptibles are maxed. After that, it’s Shma Yisrael.”

“Prayer isn’t on the contingency ladder,” Dana said. “We don’t log Shma Yisrael.”

“We should,” Yuval muttered.

Somewhere in the south, a battery farm’s state-of-charge readout flipped another percent toward empty.

Dana’s phone buzzed again. This time the preview caught the corner of her eye before she could not-look.

Ido: AI minutes dying again. 4 left. Exam tomorrow. Can you—

She turned the phone face down. The boy wanted four minutes from a country that couldn’t spare one hundred eighty megawatts. Later.

“Clock,” Ronen said.

“I see the clock.”

Dana rolled her shoulders.

“Hotline,” she said. “North. Execute 4417. Trip sequence N-12C, then N-14A. Call the opens.”

“Executing,” said North’s transmission operator.

On the wall, breakers changed state. N-12C went dark, forty-two megawatts across an industrial strip nobody on television would cry about. Frequency stuttered upward a hair—49.885—then sagged again.

“N-12C open,” North called. “Forty-two off.”

“Copy,” Dana said, pen moving.

N-14A opened. Shopping district, two small clinics already on their own diesels if their maintenance contracts weren’t lies, a water booster station with a day tank—sixty-one megawatts. The sine wave lifted, fell. Someone in the back of the control room whispered yalla and then apologized to nobody.

“N-14A open. Sixty-one.”

“Copy.”

49.87 on the big display. The slope still negative, shallower. The deficit ribbon shivered. They needed the last bite, seventy-seven megawatts. The rotation table’s third line still read N-16A.

Rotation meant every feeder took its turn in the dark. Pull one out of the line and someone else had to take its turn as well as their own.

Dana opened the feeder-substitution pane, the manual-override field that Legal swore was for “telemetry mismatch and equipment outage only.” She typed a justification meant to survive audit: N-16A deferred — local backup diesel uncertainty; substitute N-17B equiv. MW; extend N-17B rotation block to 4hr (double block). She had no idea whether the clinic’s generator worked. Her credential stamped the log before she finished reading the sentence back: badge number, timestamp, a checksum nobody in the room would open tonight.

“North,” she said into the handset. “Third feeder is N-17B, not N-16A. Same megawatts. Mark N-17B for extended block—four hours, not two. Confirm.”

Silence long enough to hear the sharav in it.

“N-17B is municipal residential,” he said. “Confirm swap off N-16A?”

“Confirmed. My credential’s on the sub. Open N-17B.”

Another silence. Then, quieter: “Copy. Opening N-17B. Extended block logged.”

Yuval’s eyes flicked to her screen, to the substitution banner, away. He said nothing.

Ronen, from the rail: “Your call, Dana. Table’s a guide.”

“I noticed,” Dana said.

The icon on N-17B flipped from blue to gray. Seventy-seven megawatts, gone. Somewhere north of here, in a street of mid-rises and a grocery that closed at nine, the lights went out and the air conditioners died in the same second. Her board had no residential telemetry. On the adjacent pane, N-16A stayed blue, soft, unbothered. Miriam’s wing would keep its cool air, and the push of the pressure mask would keep pacing her mother’s breaths through the worst of the night.

Frequency climbed.

49.89. 49.91. 49.93.

The drop eased toward zero. The EMS deficit ribbon cooled from orange toward yellow. Ronen exhaled through his nose. Yuval let out a long breath and turned it into a cough.

Dana wrote:

19:44 #4417 EXEC N-12C / N-14A / N-17B(x2) dump ~180 HOLD D-04A (Sched 1) N-16A DEFER (cred. DH) F→49.93

Her hand was steady. That seemed incorrect.

On the adjacent pane, Feeder D-04A still held its firm block in a straight line. Most big loads jittered when the island flexed—data halls, arc furnaces, even the desal plants when they were allowed to. Orion’s hold stayed flat. The recovery curve across the system was a little too clean: no hunting, no overshoot, the kind of damping a textbook promised, or a load that knew where the grid was going. She almost flagged it. Her cursor hovered over the anomaly marker on D-04A’s trace.

She let the cursor drop.

Later.

“Restore sequence?” Yuval asked quietly.

“N-12C and N-14A when reserve says so. N-17B stays open the full four. North doesn’t rotate it early.” Dana capped the pen. The thermos sat empty. On the wall the island held fifty hertz by a few hundredths.

She watched N-17B’s gray square, even when Yuval stopped hovering.

“Desk,” she said, to no one and to the tape. “Rotation amended. Orion stays lit. That’s the hierarchy.”

Nobody argued.

Day 3 · Thursday

Two evenings later the heat had got into the walls. Dana plated leftover chicken and something that had been salad yesterday, and set three places—Miriam’s still empty at the far end—because three places was what whole people did.

Ido came to the table with cheap frames pushed up in his hair, one hinge cracked. He had his polite look on for adults: bored, faintly amused. She had stopped asking him to take them off.

“Savta says the new nurse is stealing her cookies.” He sat down. “Only the good ones.”

Dana laughed. It came out the right shape. Under the table, against her thigh, her phone showed a municipal notice from a neighborhood a few kilometers north, short, local, the kind of item that ran under real news and died by morning.

An elderly resident on Rehov HaEla had been found unresponsive after the extended outage Tuesday night. Heat exposure. Name withheld pending family. The block map in the article matched N-17B’s footprint.

Ido said something about the nurse’s shoes. She nodded in the right places. N-16A had stayed lit. Miriam had slept through the night—machine, cool air, wall power—while someone else’s grandmother on Rehov HaEla had not. The override was in the log under Dana’s credential, a tidy technical act nobody in the room that night had cared about.

She locked the phone and set it beside her plate.

“Eat,” she told her son, and smiled like a person who had nothing under the table.
